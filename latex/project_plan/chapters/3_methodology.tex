\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The methodology involves a three-stage finetuning setup outlined in Section~\ref{sub:training_setup}, with the model being fine-tuned for dependency parsing in a variety of languages. The choice for dependency parsing is based on the availability of uniform datasets in a wide variety of languages through Universal Dependencies (UD)~\cite{nivre-etal-2016-universal}. The selection of languages is clarified in Section~\ref{sub:languages}, the specifics of the model are discussed in Section~\ref{sub:model}.

\subsection{Model}
\label{sub:model}
	The model used is derived from UDify,~\cite{kondratyuk-straka-2019-75} which is itself based on M-BERT~\cite{devlin-etal-2019-bert}. UDify incorporates a layer attention mechanism that scales each layer for its importance to the task at hand. That is, during fine-tuning, a weighted sum $\mathbf{r}_j$ is computed for input token $j$ over all layers $i \in [1, ..., 12]$ as follows:

	$$
		\mathbf{r}_j = \eta\sum_i\mathbf{U}_{i,j} \cdot \text{softmax}(\mathbf{\lambda})_i
	$$

	\noindent where $\mathbf{U}_{i,j}$ is the output of layer $i$ at token position $j$, $\eta$ a trainable scalar, and $\mathbf{\lambda}$ a vector of trainable scalar mixing weights. Next, $\mathbf{r}_j$ is passed through two separate biaffine attention classifiers~\cite{dozat-manning-2016-deep} for predicting the arc and the tag, respectively. Finally, the Chu-Liu/Egmonds algorithm~\cite{chu-1965-shortest} is used to obtain a valid dependency tree.

\subsection{Fine-tuning setup}
\label{sub:training_setup}
	As stated, the fine-tuning setup follows three stages:
	\begin{list}{}{\leftmargin=1em \rightmargin=1em}
		\item
		\begin{enumerate}[label=Stage \arabic*:, leftmargin=*, align=left]
		    \item The base model \mbase is finetuned on the a treebank in the source language, German, to instill general nkowledge of the task at hand, the result is \mde;
		    \item \mde is finetuned separately on treebanks of each of the auxiliary languages $\ell \in \{\textit{nld}, \textit{nor}, \textit{ces}, \textit{fin}\}$\footnote{Dutch, Norwegian, Czech, and Finnish.} to obtain four instances of $M_\ell$;
		    \item \mde and each $M_\ell$ is finetuned on each of the target languages $\lambda \in \{\textit{gsw},$ $\textit{fao},$ $\textit{hsb},$ $\textit{vep}\}$,\footnote{Swiss German, Faroese, Upper Sorbian, and Veps.} resulting in 4 instances of \m{\textit{\scriptsize deu}}{\lambda} and 16 instances of \m{\ell}{\lambda}.
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{list}

	So far, preliminary experiments have shown most updates to occur primarily in the classifier head and not in the M-BERT base. Given that the focus of this thesis is the cross-lingual generalizability of M-BERT, if the answer to \textbf{Q3} is that M-BERT's embedding space remains largely unchanged throughout finetuning, that would more likely be the result of the particular setup used than of any property inherent to M-BERT. Therefore, two setups are ran alongside each other. In one, the classifier is updated in all finetuning steps. In the other, the classifier head is frozen after random initialization. The latter setup forces all updates to M-BERT's parameters as its final embedding has to to encode all information needed for a fully language-neutral, untrained classifier to parse dependencies in a given language. Should either setup result in findings that do not answer the research questions, it will be dropped in the thesis.

\subsection{Languages}
\label{sub:languages}
	Broadly speaking, language selection is based on their typological similarity and their prevalence in M-BERT's pre-training data as reported by Wu \& Dredze~\cite{wu-dredze-2020-languages}. Typological similarity is defined using the syntactic feature-vectors (\texttt{syntax\_knn}) from the URIEL knowledge base~\cite{littell-etal-2017-uriel}. In particular, each language is represented as a feature-vector, and similarity between languages is taken to be the cosine similarity between their respective vectors.

	German was selected as one of the most prominent languages in M-BERT's pre-training data. Moreover, Turc et al.~\cite{turc-etal-2021-revisiting} found German (alongside Russian) to be the most effective language for cross-lingual transfer. The selection of the auxiliary languages is motivated phylogenetically, as they each represent a different level of relatedness to German (Finnish is unrelated, Czech is in the same family, Norwegian is in the same branch, Dutch is in the same subbranch). However, a hierarchical clustering analysis of the \texttt{syntax\_knn} vectors (Appendix~\ref{app:a}) found their selection to be typologically valid, too. The target languages were selected as being the most typologically similar language to each of the auxiliary languages that (a) have a UD treebank and (b) are not part of M-BERT's pretraining data. For Dutch, the most similar low-resource language was found to be Swiss German, Norwegian was matched with Faroese, Czech with Upper Sorbian, and Finnish with Veps.

	For hyperparameter tuning, out of all languages with a treebank with more than 10\,000 examples, the one whose URIEL vector is most equidistant to each of the other language vectors is used. This was found to be Portuguese. An overview of the languages, as well as the specific treebanks used is given in Appendix~\ref{app:b}.


% For the final set of languages, the most similar languages from a selection of low-resource languages included in UD were found for each of the intermediary languages. For Dutch and Swedish, these were found to be Swiss German and Faroese, respectively. However, the similarity between those pairs was found to be considerably higher than the similarity between Czech, Hungarian, and their respective most similar languages\,---\,Upper Sorbian and Veps. Since the cosine similarity between Dutch and Faroese was found to be more comparable, the decision was made to disregard Swedish in the final stage and to match Dutch with Faroese.
