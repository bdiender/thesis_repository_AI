\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
	By training for next-word prediction in large datasets of texts, language models learn to encode linguistic properties that can be leveraged in a wide variety of downstream language-related tasks. In particular, transformer-based language models have become a cornerstone technology in Natural Language Processing (NLP) by advancing the state-of-the-art in many NLP tasks. However, their data-hungry nature limits their applicability to the few languages that have sufficiently large datasets for language models to train on.

	One solution to this limitation is to have language models train on texts written in a variety of languages. By doing so, such models pick up on near-universal linguistic properties, such as there being a distinction between nouns and verbs~\cite{evans-2009-myth}, as well as properties shared only by subsets of languages, such as verbs preceding their subject in a sentence. This information is then encoded in a shared semantic space that allows multilingual language models to use information obtained from one language when processing another in a process called cross-lingual transfer. This is particularly useful in the low-resource setting: a language model may not have trained on enough data in Scottish Gaelic to have captured that verbs precede their subject in the language. However, if the model has been trained on enough texts in Modern Standard Arabic, it may well have managed to encode that verbs precede their subjects in that language, and consequently transfer that knowledge cross-lingually to better deal with texts in Scottish Gaelic.

	In a process called zero-shot transfer, Pires et al.~\cite{pires-etal-2019-multilingual} finetune the multilingual language model M-BERT~\cite{devlin-etal-2019-bert} on a source language and evaluate it on a different target language. They report remarkably high performance on the target languages, indicating that multilingual models can be the basis of successful classification models even when no training data is available in a given language. Lauscher et al.~\cite{lauscher-etal-2020-zero} report considerable improvements over zero-shot transfer when first finetuning on as few as 10 examples in a target language. Moreover, they find M-BERT's cross-lingual generalizability to be closely linked to how well-represented a target language is in M-BERT's pre-training data. Both Lauscher et al.~\cite{lauscher-etal-2020-zero} and Pires et al.~\cite{pires-etal-2019-multilingual} report cross-lingual transfer to be more successful when the source and target languages are typologically similar.

	Linguistic typology is the study of which linguistic properties are exhibited by which languages~\cite{croft-2002-typology}. Choenni \& Shutova~\cite{choenni-shutova-2022-investigating} investigate how typology is encoded in the embedding space of M-BERT, among others. They show that the model carves its embedding space into language-specific subspaces during pre-training. That is, sentence embeddings in a given language tend to cluster around a language-specific centroid. Moreover, probing experiments reveal that typological properties are encoded jointly across languages. For example, subtracting the Modern Standard Arabic centroid from a Scottish Gaelic sentence embedding would remove the information from the embedding that verbs precede their subject. This indicates overlapping subspaces for typologically similar languages, which could be what underlies the relation between typological similarity and successful cross-lingual transfer.

	The typologically-informed overlap between language-specific subspaces in a language model's embedding space could be what underlies cross-lingual transfer, especially since cross-lingual transfer seems to be more successful for typologically similar languages. The goal of this project is to examine that intuition. This is done in a three-stage setup where the model is consecutively finetuned to a high-resource source language, one of various auxiliary languages, and finally for a few examples to one of various low-resource target languages. The specific aim is then to answer the following questions:

	\begin{list}{}{\leftmargin=1em \rightmargin=1em}
		\item
		\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Q\arabic*:}, leftmargin=*, align=left]
		    \item Does greater typological similarity between the source and target languages result in better zero-shot cross-lingual transfer performance?
		    \item To what degree does few-shot learning improve performance over zero-shot transfer?
		    \item Is few-shot transfer from a source to a target language more effective when first fine-tuning on a more typologically similar auxiliary language?
		    \item Does fine-tuning on a language make its typological features more easily identifiable in the model's embedding space?
		\end{enumerate}
	\end{list}

In short, language models are trained for next-word prediction in large datasets of text and, in doing so, pick up on linguistic properties that can be leveraged in a wide variety of downstream language-related tasks. Multilingual language models (MLMs) are trained on texts in many different languages. In the process, they pick up on both linguistic properties that are shared across almost all languages, such as there being a distinction between nouns and verbs~\cite{evans-2009-myth}; as well as language-specific properties shared by only a subset of all languages, such as verbs encoding tense. The result is that an MLM can bootstrap knowledge obtained from one language when processing another in a process called \textbf{cross-lingual transfer}. This is particularly useful for languages that lack the data to adequately pick up on the language's patterns. For example, a language model may not have trained on enough data in Scottish Gaelic to know that verbs precede their subject in the language. However, it may well have knowledge of verbs preceding their subjects in Modern Standard Arabic and transfer that knowledge cross-lingually to better deal with Scottish Gaelic.
